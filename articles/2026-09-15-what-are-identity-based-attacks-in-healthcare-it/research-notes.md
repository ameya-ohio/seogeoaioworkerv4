# Research Notes: What Are Identity-Based Attacks in Healthcare IT

## Topic Summary

Identity-based attacks are intrusions in which an adversary uses a legitimate-looking digital identity — a stolen password, a synced service account, a forged Kerberos ticket, a hijacked authentication session — to gain and escalate access, rather than relying primarily on malware or a software exploit. CrowdStrike frames the scale of the shift starkly: "5 of the top 10 MITRE ATT&CK tactics are identity based." In healthcare IT this pattern is now the dominant path into a hospital network, and the risk is structurally worse than in a typical enterprise because most health systems do not run a single, clean identity plane. They run on-premises Active Directory (AD) — often decades old, wired into EHR, PACS/imaging, and lab-integration systems that cannot easily be re-platformed — synchronized to Microsoft Entra ID (Azure AD) for email, collaboration, and cloud apps. Hospitals sit at the leading edge of that pattern because clinical systems create some of the deepest, hardest-to-migrate AD dependencies of any industry.

The topic matters right now because the industry's reference incident — the February 2024 Change Healthcare/UnitedHealth Group ransomware attack — was a textbook identity-based attack: stolen Citrix credentials, no MFA on that portal, and roughly nine days of undetected lateral movement before ransomware deployment, disrupting claims processing nationwide. Since then, Microsoft's own incident-response team has documented threat actors (Storm-0501) doing exactly what the "hybrid seam" framing predicts: compromising on-prem AD first, pivoting through a second, compromised Entra Connect server, identifying a non-human synced identity holding Global Administrator rights with no MFA registered, resetting its password on-prem (which synced to the cloud via Password Hash Sync), and registering a new MFA method the attacker controlled — full cloud tenant compromise starting from an on-prem foothold (Microsoft Security Blog, August 27, 2025). A July 2026 Identity Theft Resource Center report (via HIPAA Journal) shows the underlying trend is not slowing: healthcare data breaches rose to 281 in the first half of 2026 from 270 in the same period a year earlier, with malicious-insider incidents jumping sevenfold (from 3 in all of 2025 to 21 in H1 2026 alone) — and reporting transparency is getting worse, with only 24% of breach notices disclosing an attack vector, the lowest rate ITRC has ever recorded. Layered on top of this, hospitals carry two identity-risk categories most horizontal identity-security content skips entirely: a massive, poorly authenticated fleet of connected medical devices (IoMT) — 99% of hospitals manage at least one IoMT device with a known exploited vulnerability (Ordr, April 2026) — and a non-human identity population (service accounts for imaging systems, integration engines, device authentication) that is proliferating faster than security teams' confidence in managing it.

Who cares: CISOs and security architects at hospitals and health systems running hybrid AD/Entra environments; compliance and risk officers tracking HIPAA exposure; boards facing scrutiny after Change Healthcare; and biomedical/clinical engineering teams who own medical devices but rarely own identity posture for them. Current discourse splits into two camps that rarely intersect: generic "identity-based attack" content (definitions, attack-type listicles, Zero Trust framing) written for a horizontal enterprise audience, and healthcare-specific breach/compliance content (HIPAA breach counts, ransomware cost figures) — with almost nothing connecting the two through the specific mechanics of the on-prem-AD-to-Azure-AD trust relationship, which is exactly the seam attackers are using.

## Debates & Counterpoints
- **Is the risk "on-prem AD vs. Azure AD," or is that framing wrong?** Vendor comparison content frames this as a binary — which platform is "more secure." Counterpoint from the technical hybrid-identity research (The Security Factory, Microsoft's own Storm-0501 writeup): the comparison is close to meaningless in a hybrid estate, because compromising either side under Password Hash Sync, Pass-Through Auth, or Federation lets an attacker reach the other. Where the evidence leans: the real risk variable isn't which platform is inherently stronger, it's the sync/trust relationship connecting them — a hardened Azure AD tenant is still exposed if its Entra Connect server or a synced Global Admin account isn't treated as Tier-0.
- **Will hospitals eventually go fully cloud-native, retiring on-prem AD?** Cloud-vendor content often implies migration is the long-term fix. Counterpoint: clinical-system vendors (imaging, lab, legacy EHR modules) frequently hard-require on-prem AD/Kerberos, and industry surveys consistently find large majorities of enterprises describing hybrid identity as their durable state, not a transitional one. Where the evidence leans: hybrid coexistence is the realistic operating model for most hospitals for the foreseeable future — security guidance premised on "eventually you'll fully migrate away from the risk" is not realistic for this audience; the seam has to be defended, not just outgrown.
- **Is MFA "the fix," or does the Change Healthcare/Storm-0501 evidence show something deeper?** Public commentary after Change Healthcare focused heavily on "there was no MFA" as the root cause. Counterpoint: in the Storm-0501 case, the compromised pivot account was a non-human synced identity that could not be enrolled in interactive MFA at all — the attacker's own move was to register a new MFA method after taking over the account, using MFA against the defender. Where the evidence leans: MFA is necessary on every human-facing entry point, but non-human/service identities need a different control model (credential rotation, least privilege, Tier-0 treatment of sync infrastructure) rather than the MFA answer that works for staff logins.
- **Is the H1 2026 rise in healthcare breaches an identity story or an insider-threat story?** ITRC's H1 2026 data frames the sevenfold jump in incidents as "malicious insider" activity, which some coverage treats as distinct from identity-based attacks. Counterpoint: an insider using their own or a hijacked account for unauthorized access is functionally an identity-based compromise (abuse of a valid identity's access), and the same falling attack-vector-disclosure rate (24%) that accompanies it makes it hard to cleanly separate "insider" from "compromised/misused identity" in the reported data. Where the evidence leans: treat the H1 2026 trend as reinforcing, not contradicting, the identity-centrality thesis — and as a reason reported breach causes may understate identity's true share.

## Content Gaps (Opportunities)
- No ranking page explains the on-prem-AD-to-Azure-AD pivot mechanism (Entra Connect Sync, Password Hash Sync, synced Global Admin accounts) using a named, dated incident (Storm-0501, 2025) *and* frames it for a hospital CISO's actual environment — the mechanism content (The Security Factory, TheHackerNews) is generic-enterprise; the healthcare content (Clearwater, DAS Health, Security Info Watch, Enzoic) never crosses into Azure AD/hybrid mechanics at all.
- Medical device/EHR identity exposure (Ordr's 99%-vulnerable data; device-per-bed density data) is covered only in device-security-vendor content, never connected to the AD/Azure AD identity graph — no one explains, for a hospital security architect, how a compromised imaging-system service account authenticates into the same AD/Azure AD trust boundary as clinical staff accounts, and why that matters for attack-path reachability.
- Non-human identity risk is covered in general enterprise/DevOps-focused content but is essentially absent from healthcare-specific security writing — despite hospitals plausibly having one of the highest NHI-to-human ratios of any vertical (imaging systems, integration engines, device authentication, lab interfaces), a claim that is directionally well-supported but for which no healthcare-specific ratio figure could be independently verified this pass.
- No content found applies a reachability/propagation/impact-style prioritization framework to the specific question a hybrid healthcare security team actually faces: given hundreds of AD misconfigurations and thousands of non-human identities, which specific chokepoints, if fixed, cut off the most attack paths into Azure AD and clinical systems. Existing content offers generic hardening checklists (patch, enable MFA, restrict admin) rather than a prioritization method — this is the clearest wedge for Saporo's graph/attack-path framing.
- No 2026-dated healthcare content connects the ITRC's H1 2026 insider-incident surge to the identity-based-attack thesis — coverage treats "insider threat" and "identity-based attack" as separate categories, missing that both are ultimately about the same control gap (excess standing access, weak identity governance).

For hybrid AD/Azure AD mechanics specifically, the cluster shifts again to Microsoft's own security blog and specialist technical blogs (The Security Factory, Cayosoft) rather than mainstream security media.
- How those answers tend to frame the topic: a short generic definition ("attacks that exploit valid credentials/identity rather than malware") followed immediately by a numbered list of attack types, then — for healthcare queries — a jump straight to the Change Healthcare incident as the default illustrative example, often paired with breach-cost/breach-count statistics. Hybrid AD/Azure AD content, when it appears at all, stays in a separate, non-healthcare technical silo.
- The framing AI engines are NOT using yet (potential wedge): no observed answer combines the hybrid on-prem-AD/Azure-AD trust relationship as the named point of exposure, a hospital-specific incident/mechanism walkthrough (a Storm-0501-style pivot applied to a clinical environment), medical-device/EHR service-account risk, and non-human identity risk into one coherent healthcare-scoped narrative. Owning that combination — and pairing it with a reachability-based prioritization method rather than a generic checklist — is not yet a pattern AI engines default to for this topic.
