# Research Notes: Kerberos Attack Mitigation for Healthcare Organizations

## Topic Summary
Kerberos is the default authentication protocol behind on-premises Active Directory (AD), and its ticket-based trust model is one of the highest-value attack surfaces in enterprise IT — especially in healthcare, where hospitals run large numbers of long-lived, high-privilege service accounts tied to EHR platforms, PACS imaging, lab integration engines, and clinical middleware. Four related attack techniques dominate the discourse: Kerberoasting (offline-cracking service-account passwords obtained via TGS tickets), Golden Ticket attacks (forging TGTs after compromising the KRBTGT account), Pass-the-Hash / Pass-the-Ticket (replaying stolen hashes or tickets for lateral movement), and delegation abuse (exploiting unconstrained, constrained, or resource-based constrained Kerberos delegation to impersonate arbitrary users). None of these are new — Kerberoasting was named in 2014 and Golden Ticket research dates to 2014–2015 — but they became acutely urgent in healthcare after the May 2024 Ascension Health ransomware attack, in which attackers who gained initial access via a contractor's malware-infected laptop used Kerberoasting to compromise a domain-admin-privileged service account protected only by weak, RC4-encrypted credentials, ultimately disrupting clinical operations and — per the HHS Office for Civil Rights breach portal, updated December 19, 2024 — exposing records for 5,599,699 people.

This topic matters right now for four converging reasons. First, healthcare has held the highest average data-breach cost of any industry for 14 consecutive years, and its breach identify-and-contain time (279 days) is the slowest of any industry (IBM/HIPAA Journal, 2025) — giving attackers who gain a foothold via Kerberos abuse a long runway before detection. Second, the Ascension breach became a political flashpoint: in September 2025, Senator Ron Wyden formally asked the FTC to investigate Microsoft for "gross cybersecurity negligence" over its continued default support for the RC4 encryption that enabled the Kerberoasting in that attack, revealing that his staff had urged Microsoft as early as July 29, 2024 to warn customers, but Microsoft did not publish mitigation guidance until October 11, 2024. Third — and this is the freshest, most publish-worthy development — Microsoft has since followed through: a phased rollout tied to CVE-2026-20833 moved from an audit-only phase (January 2026) to enforced AES-only defaults with manual rollback (April 2026) to full enforcement with rollback removed entirely (July 2026). As of this writing (September 2026), RC4 is disabled by default across current Active Directory domain controllers unless an admin explicitly re-enables it — which flips the healthcare problem from "wait for Microsoft" to "our legacy clinical systems (older PACS boxes, lab middleware, imaging modalities) may already be failing Kerberos authentication, and the compensating option Microsoft itself discourages (explicitly re-enabling RC4) creates its own HIPAA technical-safeguards exposure." Fourth, most health systems still run identity in hybrid form — on-prem AD federated or synced to Entra ID — so a Kerberos compromise on-prem routinely has a cloud-identity blast radius that generic guidance doesn't address.

Discourse today splits along three lines: (1) generic AD-security vendors (CrowdStrike, Netwrix, BeyondTrust, Cymulate, Cayosoft, Rapid7, Vectra, Fidelis) treat Kerberos attacks as discrete, individually patchable techniques with a checklist of controls; (2) government/standards and vendor-primary sources (CISA, MITRE ATT&CK, Microsoft Learn/Tech Community) supply the authoritative technical mitigation baseline and the 2026 RC4-retirement mechanics but are not healthcare-specific; (3) a small number of healthcare-vertical pieces (e.g., AccountableHQ, PresideTech/Preside) translate generic guidance into hospital-system or HIPAA-compliance language but stop at implementation checklists rather than connecting the techniques into an attack-path narrative or addressing what happens to legacy clinical systems now that RC4 enforcement is live.

## Debates & Counterpoints
- **Whose responsibility was RC4 exposure — the vendor's or the customer's?** Senator Wyden's September 2025 letter argued Microsoft bore primary responsibility because it controlled the default encryption setting and customers rarely changed it in practice, while Microsoft's own guidance framed RC4 exposure as something customers should proactively remediate today, independent of any eventual default change. Where the evidence currently lands: both mattered, but the timeline resolved the ambiguity — Microsoft's phased 2026 rollout (audit in January, enforcement in April, no-rollback in July) means the vendor default is now fixed; the residual risk has shifted entirely to organizations that haven't remediated legacy accounts and are tempted to explicitly re-enable RC4 as a workaround.
- **Now that RC4 enforcement is live, is explicitly re-enabling it for legacy device compatibility a reasonable compensating control?** Microsoft's own materials call explicit RC4 re-enablement "strongly discouraged" but leave the door open as a technical option; healthcare-compliance commentary (PresideTech) argues that doing so recreates the exact HIPAA §164.312 technical-safeguards exposure the industry just spent 2026 remediating, especially given NIST's formal RC4 deprecation (SP 800-131A Rev. 2). Where the evidence leans: treat explicit RC4 re-enablement as a time-boxed, documented exception tied to a hardware/software replacement plan, not a standing configuration — generic vendor content doesn't yet address this because most of it predates the July 2026 enforcement date.
- **Is Event ID 4769 monitoring actually a reliable detection method?** Vendor content (Netwrix) presents Event ID 4769 monitoring as a core detection pillar, but DFIR research (NCC Group) shows raw 4769 volume is noisy because legitimate TGS requests happen constantly; the more precise signal is filtering on the RC4 ticket-encryption-type flag (0x17) combined with anomalous per-user request volume, not raw event counts. Where the evidence leans: encryption-type filtering plus behavioral baselining is more defensible than raw event-count alerting, but most generic vendor content doesn't clearly make this distinction.
- **Is patching/checklist-based mitigation sufficient, or does exposure require path-level prioritization?** Nearly every top-ranking piece (CrowdStrike, Netwrix, BeyondTrust, AccountableHQ, Rapid7) treats Kerberoasting, Golden Ticket, and delegation abuse as independently patchable line items. ITDR-focused research (Semperis, NCC Group) increasingly frames these techniques as connected steps on an attack path — a kerberoastable account is only catastrophic because of what it's delegated to reach, and delegation abuse is what turns a single compromised host into domain-wide compromise. Where the evidence leans: the connected/attack-path framing better matches how the Ascension and CISA RVA incidents actually unfolded (a single lower-value account led to domain admin), but it is largely absent from the generic top-ranking content — a clear content gap.
